How to Prepare a Custom Bag Tech Pack

  • Company News

Posted by Trimela On Aug 04 2026

A bag may look simple when it is finished, but a factory has to make hundreds of connected decisions before that result exists. How wide is the gusset? Where does the zipper stop? Is the handle length measured end to end or as a drop? Which side of the fabric faces outward? What happens at a stress point? A custom bag tech pack answers those questions before cutting begins.

This guide explains how to prepare a custom bag tech pack that a sample room, costing team and production line can actually use. It is written for brands, importers, promotional product distributors and product managers developing totes, backpacks, cosmetic bags, cooler bags, drawstring bags and related sewn products. If you are still evaluating production partners, first review our Custom Bag Manufacturer: Complete OEM/ODM Buyer’s Guide. If the bag format is not yet fixed, the product range can help you define the starting category.

A useful tech pack connects technical drawings, material references, trims and approved samples in one controlled specification.

What Is a Custom Bag Tech Pack?

A tech pack is the controlled set of instructions used to quote, sample, manufacture and inspect a product. For bags, it normally combines drawings, dimensions, materials, components, construction methods, artwork, labels, packaging and quality expectations. It is not merely a mood board, a photograph or a page of general notes.

The strongest tech packs remove avoidable interpretation without pretending that product development is already finished. Early versions can contain open points, but those points should be visible, assigned and resolved through sampling. The document then becomes a record of decisions rather than a collection of scattered messages.

Document Primary purpose What it cannot replace

Design brief Explains the user, use case, price position and creative intent Detailed production instructions

Tech pack Defines the product in measurable and reviewable terms A physical proof of fit, feel and workmanship

Costing sheet Records unit cost, tooling,packaging and commercial assumptions Technical drawings or construction standards

Approved sample Shows the accepted physical result Written tolerances, color records and packing instructions

Purchase order Confirms quantity, price, delivery and commercial terms The complete product specification

Why the Tech Pack Matters Before Quotation

A factory can quote from a photograph, but the number will be based on assumptions. Two suppliers may interpret the same image differently: one may allow a lightweight lining, another a heavier one; one may price a basic zipper, another a branded-grade component. The quotations appear comparable even though the products are not.

A structured pack creates a common basis.It also helps the manufacturer identify cost drivers, feasibility problems and missing information early. That makes the first quotation more useful and reduces the chance of discovering a major cost change after the sample is approved. For commercial planning, compare the specification with our guides to typical custom bag MOQs and custom bag cost drivers.

Without a controlled tech pack With a controlled tech pack

Prices depend heavily on each supplier’s assumptions Suppliers cost the same defined product

Sample comments are spread across chats and email threads Revisions are recorded against one version

Dimensions are interpreted from photos Measurement points and tolerances are visible

Approved details can disappear during bulk production The production team has a signed reference

Inspection becomes subjective Inspectors can compare goods with stated criteria

The Core Sections of a Bag Tech Pack

There is no single universal layout, but the following sections work well for most sewn bags. Keep each section concise enough for production staff to use at a workstation. A longer document is not automatically a clearer document.

Section Information to include Common mistake

Cover and control page Style name, item code, owner, date, revision and status Sending files with no revision history

Product overview Use case, target user, capacity and performance priorities Describing appearance but not function

Technical drawings Front, back, sides, top, bottom,interior and detail views Showing only a front view

Measurements Numbered points, measurement method, finished dimensions and tolerance Listing dimensions without explaining where to measure

Bill of materials Shell, lining, foam, webbing, zippers, hardware, thread and reinforcements Using vague terms such as “good-quality zipper”

Construction Seam type, stitch details, binding,reinforcement and assembly notes Assuming a sample-room method will be remembered in production

Color and artwork Color standard, placement, size, technique and approved files Approving color from an uncalibrated screen alone

Labels and packaging Label content, position, folding, protection, carton and assortment Leaving packaging until production is almost finished

Quality requirements Critical dimensions, workmanship, tests and defect rules Using “high quality” as the only standard

Step 1: Establish Document Control

Start with an item code and a revision system. A simple sequence such as V0.1, V0.2 and V1.0 is enough if everyone follows it. Put the version and issue date on every page, not only the cover. Mark the working status clearly: concept, quotation, sample revision, approved for production or superseded.

One person on the buyer’s side should own the master file. The factory may annotate it, but final decisions should return to the master rather than creating parallel specifications. When a revision is issued, summarize what changed. This saves the factory from comparing entire documents line by line.

Revision Appropriate use Release rule

V0.x Concept, feasibility and initial costing Open points may remain but must be identified

V1.x Sample development Each issue records sample comments and decisions

Production issue Bulk material booking and manufacturing All critical specifications approved by authorized people

As-produced record Reorders and future improvements Reflects approved production changes and final packaging

Step 2: Draw Every View the Factory Needs

Technical drawings do not need to be artistic. They need to be unambiguous. Use clean line drawings with consistent proportions and numbered callouts. Show front and back, both sides when they differ, the base, the top closure and the interior. Add enlarged detail views for complicated pockets, strap anchors, zipper garages, piping or layered panels.

Distinguish visible stitching from panel edges and fold lines. When left and right are mirrored, say so. When they are not, show both. If a component is hidden in the finished bag—such as a reinforcement patch—use a cutaway or exploded view. The objective is to let the pattern maker understand design intent before guessing.

Group materials, pattern references, trims and measurement tools so that each callout can be traced to a physical component.

Step 3: Build a Measurement Specification

Finished bag measurements are often affected by foam, binding, curves and the way a soft product is placed on the table. For that reason, a dimension without a measurement method is incomplete.Give every point a code, such as A for finished width and B for finished height, then show the same code on the drawing and measurement table.

State whether the bag is measured empty, lightly filled or flattened. Explain whether handles are measured as total length or vertical drop. Set realistic tolerances according to product type and construction. A rigid molded case and a soft cotton tote should not automatically share the same tolerance.

Point of measure Define in the tech pack Potential ambiguity

Finished width Top, center or base width; seam-to-seam or edge-to-edge A tapered bag has several valid widths

Finished height Base seam to top edge,excluding or including trim A curved opening changes the reference point

Depth/gusset Finished depth at a named location Binding and foam affect the visible result

Handle drop Vertical distance from top edge to underside of handle Often confused with cut length

Pocket position Distance from fixed seams or centerline Measuring from a movable edge causes variation

Logo positionArtwork center relative to defined product reference lines A visual center may differ from geometric center

Step 4: Create a Usable Bill of Materials

The bill of materials, or BOM, names every material and component that goes into the bag. Describe each item with enough information to source or verify it. For fabric, include fiber or material type, construction, weight or thickness, finish, color reference and any performance requirement. For hardware, include material, finish, size, shape and functional expectation.

A reference sample can help communicate hand feel or appearance, but record what the sample is proving. Is it the color, coating, stiffness, texture or complete construction? Otherwise, different teams may use the same reference for different purposes.

BOM item Useful specification fields Evidence to approve

Shell fabric Composition, weave/knit, weight, finish, color and usable width Supplier swatch or approved cutting

Lining Composition, weight, coating, color and opacity Lining swatch in the intended color

Foam or padding Material, thickness,density and location Cross-section or padded panel sample

Zipper Type, gauge, tape color, slider, puller and length Physical zipper and puller set

Webbing Fiber, width, thickness, weave, color and breaking requirement if relevant Cut length from proposed production lot

Hardware Component drawing, internal width, material, finish and color Physical sample or signed reference

Thread Fiber,ticket size, color and use location Stitch sample on actual material

Reinforcement Material, size and exact hidden position Construction sample or cutaway photo

Step 5: Specify Construction, Not Just Appearance

Two bags can look similar but perform differently because of what is inside the seams. Use detail callouts for seam allowance, binding, edge paint, folded edges, topstitching, bartacks, box stitches and hidden reinforcement. Identify stitch rows and important stitch distances where appearance or strength depends on them.

Focus first on high-risk areas: handle attachments, shoulder-strap anchors, zipper ends, base corners and openings that receive repeated force. Invite the factory to propose a production-friendly alternative when the requested method is difficult, but require that alternative to be documented and approved before it replaces the original requirement.

Risk area Tech-pack instruction to consider What to verify on the sample

Handle attachment Reinforcement size, stitch pattern and position Load path, symmetry and no exposed reinforcement

Zipper opening Finished length, end treatment and seam clearance Smooth travel without catching the lining

Bound seam Binding material,finished width and overlap location No missed edges, puckering or excessive bulk

Padded panel Foam type, quilting or retention method and edge reduction Even feel and clean seam turn

Base corner Notch, dart or boxed-corner method Matching shape and left-right consistency

Step 6: Control Color, Branding and Artwork

Separate product color from artwork color. Reference an accepted color system, a physical standard or an approved material swatch, and identify which reference takes priority if they disagree. Different substrates reflect light differently, so an ink on cotton may not visually match molded plastic hardware even when both target the same code.

For every logo, state the decoration method, finished size, position, orientation and color.Attach production-ready vector artwork where possible, and include a visual placement drawing. Define whether the dimensions refer to total artwork, the visible mark or the printing plate. Embroidery also needs thread, stitch direction and backing decisions; woven labels need fold type, edge treatment and sewing position.

Branding method Tech-pack details Sample approval focus

Screen print Ink type, color, print size, location and opacity expectation Coverage, edge definition, adhesion and hand feel

Heat transfer Film type, artwork size, position and pressing limits Edge lift, color, surface change and durability

Embroidery Thread reference, size, stitch file,backing and placement Density, distortion, puckering and small-detail clarity

Woven label Yarn colors, dimensions, fold, cut edge and sewing method Legibility, alignment and edge comfort

Metal badge Material, finish, logo relief, fixing method and protective backing Finish consistency, security and damage to contents

Step 7: Add Labels, Packaging and Carton Instructions

Packaging influences both cost and product condition. Define hangtags, barcode labels, care or origin labels, tissue, inserts, dust bags, polybags and carton packing early enough to include them in costing and compliance review. Show where each label is placed and who supplies the artwork or data.

State the folding method, whether straps are secured, which hardware needs protection and how bags are arranged in the carton.If shape recovery matters, define stuffing or support. Carton dimensions may remain provisional until the final sample is available, but the target pack quantity and destination requirements should be known.

Packaging level Questions to resolve Record in the pack

On-product Which permanent labels are required and where? Artwork, material, size, language and sewing position

Individual pack How is the bag folded and protected? Folding diagram, inserts, bag type and warning requirements if applicable

Inner pack Are colors or sizes bundled? Assortment,quantity and identification label

Export carton What pack quantity protects the product and fits handling needs? Carton type, target dimensions, gross-weight limit and marks

Pallet or delivery unit Does the destination require a specific configuration? Pallet, wrap, label and routing instructions where applicable

Step 8: Translate Quality Expectations into Checks

“Good quality” is not an inspection instruction. Identify the dimensions, functions and appearance points that matter most. Define test methods when a result cannot be judged visually. Requirements should fit the product’s use, materials, target market and buyer risk assessment rather than being copied blindly from another style.

Link every critical quality check to a specification, approved sample or test requirement. Decide who will inspect, at which stage, using what sample plan or acceptance rule.The detailed Custom Bag Quality Inspection Checklist can help turn the tech pack into an inspection-ready document.

Quality topic Objective requirement example Where to record it

Dimensions Named measurement point with finished tolerance Measurement specification

Function Zipper opens fully without catching; buckle locks and releases correctly Functional-check list

Workmanship No open seams, skipped stitches,exposed raw edges or unsecured thread ends in defined areas Defect reference sheet

Appearance Logo position, panel symmetry and acceptable color reference Artwork page and approved sample

Strength Buyer-defined load or performance test appropriate to intended use Test protocol and report requirement

Packing Correct assortment, protection, barcode and carton mark Packaging specification

Step 9: Use the Sample to Close Open Points

The first sample is a working prototype, not a ceremonial approval. Review it against the tech pack point by point. Record each comment with a photo, location, requested change and priority. Distinguish a defect from a design revision: a defect fails the current specification, while a design revision changes it.

After each review, update the master pack and issue a clean revision. Do not rely on a marked sample alone, because marks can be lost and physical samples can deteriorate. Keep the final approved sample together with the final production issue. Our Custom Bag Sample Development Process explains how to organize prototype rounds and approvals in more detail.

Comment status Meaning Required action

Must correct Product does not meet an agreed critical requirement Revise before approval and show evidence

Design change Buyer is changing the previous instruction Update specification, cost and timing impact

Factory proposal Supplier suggests a method,material or tolerance change Buyer approves or rejects in writing

Accept for this sample Temporary deviation is understood but not approved for bulk Keep the production requirement unchanged

Approved Point is accepted for the next controlled stage Capture it in the master pack and approval record

A Practical Tech-Pack Workflow

A disciplined workflow keeps design, sourcing and production aligned. Build the pack early, then increase its precision as evidence becomes available. The document should mature with the product rather than being written once and forgotten.

Stage Buyer output Factory output Decision gate

Concept and range selection Brief, reference images and target parameters Feasibility questions and relevant examples Choose a workable product direction

Initial tech pack Drawings, preliminary BOM, dimensions and branding Cost assumptions,options and risk comments Authorize prototype

Sample round Consolidated review against the pack Revised sample and updated material evidence Approve or request another round

Pre-production Production issue, artwork and packaging approvals Material confirmations and production plan Release bulk manufacturing

Production and inspection Approved standards and inspection instructions Goods,in-process records and packing evidence Accept, correct or hold shipment

Reorder record As-produced pack and lessons learned Confirmed repeat specification Start the next order from controlled data

The complete custom bag manufacturing process shows where these gates fit between design and delivery. When scheduling work, also allow enough time for sample rounds, material approvals and packaging confirmation; see How Long Does Custom Bag Production Take? for a fuller planning framework.

Common Tech-Pack Mistakes

Treating a reference photo as a specification

A photo can communicate style, but it rarely reveals hidden construction, material performance or exact scale. Use it as a reference and state which features should be followed.

Mixing approved and unapproved information

Use status labels, color coding or an open-point list. Production staff should not have to guess whether a note is final.

Changing the sample without changing the document

If the pack and approved sample disagree, the next team may follow the wrong reference. Update both the written requirement and revision log.

Over-specifying the wrong details

Do not spend pages controlling a minor visual point while leaving strap strength, pocket size or packaging undefined. Rank details by user impact and commercial risk.

Ignoring cost and manufacturing feedback

A tech pack should control the intended result, but it should also permit an informed discussion. Ask the manufacturer to identify expensive, fragile or inconsistent details and document any agreed alternative.

Tech-Pack Handover Checklist

Check Ready when…

Document control Every page carries the current item code, revision and date

Views and callouts All exterior, interior and hidden critical details are shown

Measurements Each point has a method,finished value and appropriate tolerance

Materials and trims Every component has a specification and approval reference

Construction Stress areas, seams, stitches, binding and reinforcement are defined

Branding Method, artwork, size, color and placement are approved

Packaging Labels, folding, protection,assortment and carton requirements are covered

Quality Critical checks and objective acceptance references are available

Open points Nothing unresolved is presented as production-approved

Sign-off Authorized buyer and factory contacts know which issue controls production

Frequently Asked Questions

Do I need specialist software?

No. Professional design software can speed up drawings and revision control, but a well-organized PDF made from common drawing and spreadsheet tools can work. Clarity, measurement logic and version discipline matter more than the software name.

Can the manufacturer create the tech pack?

Yes, some manufacturers provide development support.The buyer should still supply design intent, use case, brand assets, target quantities and commercial priorities, then review and own the approved specification. Confirm whether drawing or development charges apply.

How detailed should the first version be?

Detailed enough to expose major cost and feasibility assumptions. Use open-point markers for information that depends on supplier advice or sampling. Do not label an incomplete document as approved for production.

Should tolerances be the same for every measurement?

No. Set them according to material behavior, construction method, product function and measurement repeatability. Discuss critical points with the manufacturer and inspector before production.

Is the approved sample enough for a reorder?

It helps, but a controlled as-produced tech pack is safer. Materials can change, samples age and undocumented production decisions are easily forgotten. Keep both the retained sample and final records.

Turn the Idea into a Factory-Ready Brief

A useful custom bag tech pack makes decisions visible. It tells the costing team what is being priced, the sample room what is being developed, production what must be repeated and inspectors what must be checked. Just as importantly, it shows which questions still need answers.
